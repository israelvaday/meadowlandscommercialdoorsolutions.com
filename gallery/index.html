Buyer's guide

How to compare commercial door estimates

A useful storefront or hardware quote lists the door, frame, closer, lock, and extras — not a one-line price.

Name the opening, not just the door A street-facing storefront, a restaurant patio door, and an office corridor door need different glass, hardware, and weather seals. The quote should say which opening is included.

Itemize the hardware Closers, push bars, locks, pivots, and access-control prep should appear as line items. Two bids are hard to compare if one hides the closer and the other does not.

Ask how the door will latch after hours A door that swings nicely but does not lock is not finished. Electric strikes, cylinders, and panic devices belong in the same conversation as the door itself.

Check code and egress hardware Public entries often need panic hardware and a closer that lets the door self-close. Corridor and stair doors may need a labeled assembly. Those requirements should be written down before work starts.

Plan for river wind and heavy use Jersey City storefronts take wind, salt air, and constant foot traffic. Thresholds, weather pile, and closer power are part of making the door last.

Confirm cleanup and the final walkthrough The finish should include removing the old door or hardware, testing the closer and lock, and walking the job against the written quote.

Glossary

Commercial door terms, explained

The hardware on a storefront or office door matters as much as the door itself. These are the terms that show up on a typical commercial quote.

Storefront Entrance — The aluminum-and-glass entry system at the front of a shop, restaurant, or lobby, including the door, frame, closer, lock, and weather seal.

Aluminum Door — A narrow-stile or wide-stile commercial door used on storefronts and office lobbies. Pivots, hinges, and lock prep have to match the existing frame.

Glass Commercial Door — A full-glass or glass-lite commercial door. Floor closers, patches, and locks need the right prep so the glass stays aligned and the door latches.

Hollow Metal Door — A steel door and frame used on offices, corridors, stairs, and back-of-house openings. Often paired with a closer, lockset, and labeled hardware.

Door Closer — The device that controls how a door swings shut. Surface, concealed, and floor closers are sized to the door weight and how often it is used.

Push Bar / Panic Hardware — Exit hardware that lets people leave quickly from a public or commercial space. It has to latch securely from the outside and release cleanly from the inside.

Access Control — Readers, electric strikes, mag locks, and request-to-exit hardware added to a commercial door so the opening still closes, latches, and meets egress rules.

Offset Pivot — A hinge-style pivot used on many aluminum storefront doors. Worn pivots make the door sag, rub the threshold, and miss the lock.
